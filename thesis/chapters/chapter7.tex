\label{chap.7}

This chapter reports the evaluations of the hybrid tester: the pre-registered held-out
evaluation on the validation suite (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}), the final tuning round on the
rebuilt development suite (Section~\ref{sec:tuning}), the external comparison with WebExplor
and WebRLED (Section~\ref{sec:extresults}), its post-hoc follow-up
(Section~\ref{sec:followup}) and its pre-registered second round
(Section~\ref{sec:round2}).

\section{Held-out evaluation (pre-registered)}
\label{sec:heldout}

\subsection{Design}

The pre-registration was frozen and its commit tagged before any agent ran on any
validation application.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Applications}: the eight validation applications (Table~\ref{tab:valsuite}),
        24 seeded faults.
  \item \textbf{Arms}:
        \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
          \item \code{random}, uniform over the page's valid actions;
          \item \code{hybrid\_nolearn}, the primary arm;
          \item \code{hybrid\_bandit};
          \item \code{v1\_dqn\_pretrained}, v1's best AC-DQN arm from Experiment 1: pretrained
                without the LLM reward and adapting online, using that experiment's own
                checkpoints. Seed $s$ loads the checkpoint of fold $s \bmod 6$, pretraining
                seed $s \bmod 3$.
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Runs}: seeds 0--4, $B = 2{,}000$ interactions, 160 hunts, plus 32 clean-build
        control runs (one per arm and application). The run took about seven and a half
        hours with four workers and no other workload.
  \item \textbf{Hypotheses} (difference in restricted mean $T$, decision rule of
        Section~\ref{sec:stats}):
        \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
          \item H1 (primary): the hybrid tester is faster than random;
          \item H2: the bandit makes the hybrid faster;
          \item H3: the hybrid with the bandit is faster than random;
          \item H4: the hybrid is faster than v1's best AC-DQN.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

One of 192 jobs failed for an infrastructure reason: Windows held a file lock while the
temporary build folder of a clean-build control was being deleted. As pre-registered, the job
was rerun unchanged; it completed with no finding.

\subsection{What the held-out data shows (descriptive)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The held-out faults were easier for random exploration than the development
        ones.} Random verified a fault in 80\% of runs, against 43\% (18 of 42) on the
        development suite. Several faults sit one or two interactions from a page that
        random visits constantly. Three faults were verified in 5 of 5 runs by every arm,
        which compresses every difference.
  \item \textbf{The hybrid arms reached the deeper faults.} 22 of the 24 faults were
        verified by at least one arm; two by none. Of the 16 faults random verified in at
        most one of five runs, the two hybrid arms together verified 14 at least once, and
        \code{v1\_dqn\_pretrained} 9. Nine faults were verified by a hybrid arm and by no
        random run. This is where test operators help: completing forms validly, invalid
        input, edits after review.
  \item \textbf{One clear blind spot: list paging.} All three skylark faults sit behind its
        feed's "Load more" button. Random presses it often by chance. The hybrid's operators
        work on forms and none pages through a list, so \code{hybrid\_nolearn} reached the
        right pages in every run but never repeated the paging (0 of 5). This cost H1 one
        of its eight applications. It was reported, not fixed, because the code was frozen;
        it motivated the \code{browse\_list} operator (Section~\ref{sec:v3-options}).
  \item \textbf{v1's pretrained AC-DQN did better here than on its own benchmark}: discovery
        0.85 and the lowest mean time, especially on dutyday and markbook. Whether that
        reflects the method or the applications' shallower faults cannot be separated with
        eight applications (H4 inconclusive).
  \item \textbf{The learner (H2).} \code{hybrid\_bandit} found a fault in more runs than
        \code{hybrid\_nolearn} (34 against 30) and more distinct faults (19 against 18), with
        a slightly higher mean time (769 against 737). As in development, the learner is
        not distinguishable from no learner.
  \item \textbf{Zero false positives.} No clean-build control verified a seeded fault
        (0 of 32 runs).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Specification-free invariants.} As pre-registered, they are reported separately.
They flagged no seeded fault for any arm. On clean builds they produced three reproduced
candidates (two for \code{hybrid\_nolearn}, one for \code{hybrid\_bandit}), all on
quizcraft's editor, all of the form "an entered value is gone after reload". On inspection,
quizcraft keeps its working copy in page memory until the user saves, so losing unsaved
edits on reload is intended. They are false alarms: the reload invariant cannot tell saved
state from unsaved state.

\section{Tuning round v3 on the rebuilt development suite}
\label{sec:tuning}

\subsection{Design}

After the held-out evaluation, the development suite was rebuilt (Section~\ref{sec:devsuite})
and the tester re-tuned on it before the external comparison. Eight arms ran on the eight
development applications with seeds 0--2, 192 runs of 2{,}000 interactions:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item the references: \code{random}, \code{hybrid\_nolearn}, \code{hybrid\_bandit} and
        \code{v1\_dqn\_pretrained};
  \item one option each (Section~\ref{sec:v3-options}):
        \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
          \item \code{dev\_v3\_browse},
          \item \code{dev\_v3\_clickables},
          \item \code{dev\_v3\_curiosity},
          \item \code{dev\_v3\_failfeedback}.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Each run records verified faults (including crash-type faults), unique failures and
JavaScript coverage. These are development data: they choose the configuration to freeze,
and are not evidence of generalisation.

\paragraph{Reading.} The \code{browse\_list} operator helped consistently but modestly: it
was faster on six of seven applications, slower on one, and lost no fault. Failure feedback
gave the best restricted mean time (450). Custom clickables did not help; they were never
faster and verified fewer distinct faults. The bandit was slower than no learner on seven of
eight applications, and its interval excludes zero. Curiosity Q-learning triggered the most
unique browser errors (5.67 per run) but verified fewer seeded faults. Random exploration
was strong here, finding a fault in 23 of 24 runs and triggering many errors, yet it
verified only one crash-type fault. The rebuilt applications are also hard: no arm verified
15 of the 41 faults.

\subsection{Configuration chosen}

The two options that did not hurt any measure, \code{browse\_list} and failure feedback,
were combined (\code{dev\_v3\_combined}) and checked in 24 further runs on the same
applications and seeds before freezing. The combination found a fault in 22 of 24 runs,
with restricted mean $T$ 465. It verified 22 of the 41 faults, more than any single arm, and
4 of the 8 crash-type faults. Against \code{hybrid\_nolearn} it was faster on six of seven
applications (median $-52$, interval $[-145, 0]$). It was frozen as \code{hybrid\_final},
and the commit was tagged before anyone looked at the external benchmark's applications.
Custom clickables and the bandit were not adopted.

\section{Comparison with WebExplor and WebRLED}
\label{sec:extresults}

\subsection{Design}

The WebRLED paper~\cite{gu2025webrled} reports results on WebExplor's six subject
applications (Dimeshift, Pagekit, Splittypie, Phoenix, Retroboard and Petclinic), with
one-hour runs and five repetitions. WebExplor averaged 55.54\% client-side branch coverage
and 3.1 failures; WebRLED averaged 64.18\% and 4.1. Coverage was measured with Istanbul on
the instrumented client code. Failures were console errors, deduplicated by hand.

The comparison was pre-registered when \code{hybrid\_final} was frozen, before anyone
looked at the subject applications.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Applications.} The same six applications, from the Docker images of the
        DIG replication material~\cite{biagiola2019diversity}, which WebExplor's benchmark
        reuses. Each image instruments its client JavaScript with Istanbul before the
        application starts.
  \item \textbf{Arms.} \code{hybrid\_final}; the hybrid with curiosity Q-learning, the RL
        arm closest to WebExplor and WebRLED; and random.
  \item \textbf{Runs.} One hour of wall-clock time per run, three repetitions (seeds 0--2),
        and a fresh application instance per run, so $3 \times 6 \times 3 = 54$ runs.
  \item \textbf{Measures.} Branch coverage of the instrumented client code, read from
        Istanbul's counters after every step and unioned over the run; and unique failures,
        meaning console errors, page errors and failed requests, deduplicated automatically
        by kind, page and normalised message.
  \item \textbf{Analysis.} A descriptive comparison with the published averages, with no
        significance test against numbers from other machines and runs, and a per-application
        comparison between our arms.
\end{itemize}

Two decisions were made during set-up and recorded before any run. First, Pagekit and
Phoenix start with a sign-in macro using their documented accounts. Second, requests to a
development live-reload server and to web analytics, which the images' pages make, are
answered with an empty response. Left alone, they would add the same console failure to
every run of every arm. Two differences from the published protocol remain and are
reported: three repetitions instead of five, and automatic instead of manual deduplication
of failures.

To finish in time, the runs were split across two machines, decided and recorded before any
result existed. Dimeshift, Pagekit and Splittypie ran on the development laptop; Phoenix,
Retroboard and Petclinic ran on four AWS spot instances (2 vCPU, 8 GB). Every run of a given
application used the same machine type, so arms are compared on equal hardware within each
application. The AWS applications also ran on GitHub Actions as a replication. Which runs
count was fixed in advance by completion, never by result. After launch, Phoenix's sign-in
was found to complete about a second after the macro ended, so episodes began on a stale
page. The macro was fixed, and every earlier Phoenix run was discarded unread.

discarded on every platform without being read, as recorded in the pre-registration.

\paragraph{Between our arms.} Both hybrid arms reached more code than random on average (44.3\%
and 44.7\% against 40.3\%). The per-application difference is small and its interval
includes zero, for \code{hybrid\_final} (median $+0.9$ points, $[-1.8, +12.9]$) and for
\code{hybrid\_curiosity} ($+2.3$, $[-0.2, +11.1]$). The curiosity arm triggered more distinct
failures than random on every application where they differed (post-hoc count: median
$+0.7$, three applications higher, none lower), consistent with its development-round
behaviour. With six applications these comparisons are descriptive.

\paragraph{Replication.} On GitHub's runners (4 vCPU, 16 GB), Phoenix and Retroboard gave
exactly the same coverage as on AWS for every arm, and the same post-hoc failure counts.
Petclinic, which has only 20 branches, varied as much between platforms as between seeds:
for \code{hybrid\_final}, 35.0\% against 46.7\%.

\paragraph{Against the published numbers.} The frozen tester does not reach the published
averages. It is comparable on Pagekit and Splittypie, below WebExplor and WebRLED on
Dimeshift, Phoenix, Retroboard and Petclinic, and below both on average (44.3\% against
55.5\% and 64.2\%; 1.8 failures against 3.1 and 4.1).

\paragraph{Why the gap: the action space (exploratory).} On Phoenix and Retroboard, no run
of any arm, random included, went beyond the same coverage: 48.7\% and 53.2\%. Both hybrid
arms reached exactly that in every run. If three different exploration strategies, one of
them random, all stop at the same branch, the limit is more likely to be what they can act
on than how they choose. Phoenix is a React application whose controls are mostly
clickable \code{div} elements. The frozen tester offers only standard HTML controls,
because the custom-clickables option (Section~\ref{sec:v3-options}) was not adopted in
tuning. The post-hoc follow-up in Section~\ref{sec:followup} tests this directly. WebRLED
learns which elements are actionable with a trained discriminator~\cite{gu2025webrled}, and
on these applications that part of its design may matter more than its choice of policy.

\section{Post-hoc follow-up: the action space}
\label{sec:followup}

The external result suggested that the tester's action space, not its policy, limited
coverage. To measure this, all three external arms were run again with custom clickables in
the action space. That is the only change; tag \code{v4-clickables} marks it. Every run of
this follow-up, with and without clickables, ran on GitHub's runners. For Dimeshift, Pagekit
and Splittypie the arms without clickables were run there anew; for the other three, the

\section{External comparison, round 2 (pre-registered)}
\label{sec:round2}

\paragraph{Design.} The first round was below the published numbers, and its diagnosis
pointed at clickable-element detection. A second round measured an improved tester under a
protocol closer to the papers'. It was pre-registered and the code tagged
(\code{v5-freeze}) before any run. Because it is informed by round 1, it is not an
independent test, and it is reported beside round 1, not in its place.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Tester (v5).} The two hybrid arms with \emph{listener-based} clickable
        detection. A script injected before the page's own code records every element that
        receives a click-type event listener. It also reads the handlers of React, Vue and
        jQuery, AngularJS and Ember action attributes, and links without an address. It was
        developed and checked only on the project's own applications.
  \item \textbf{Arms.} \code{hybrid\_final\_v5} and \code{hybrid\_curiosity\_v5}. We chose
        not to include a random arm in this round.
  \item \textbf{Protocol.} The same six applications, images, sign-in macros and stubs as
        round 1; one-hour runs; \textbf{five repetitions}, as in the papers; every run on
        the same hardware (GitHub-hosted runners, 4 vCPU, 16 GB).
\end{itemize}
All 60 runs completed; none failed.

Reading across: adding structure to random raised the distinct faults verified on the
held-out applications from 13 to 18. Adding a learner to the structure changed it by at most
one fault in either direction, and in tuning it lowered it. The deep Q-network without the
structure was fastest to a first fault on the held-out applications, but no comparison of
speed reached a verdict.

\paragraph{What the structure contributes.} Across every evaluation, the parts of the hybrid
tester that are not learned did the measurable work. Remembering states and returning to
them exactly, abstracting pages, and applying test-design operators let it verify deep
faults that random exploration did not reach: nine faults no random run found on the
held-out applications, and the most distinct faults of any configuration in the final tuning
round (22 of 41). The verified-fault oracle produced no false positive in any clean-build
control.

\paragraph{What learning contributes.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Deep RL (v1).} Pretraining gave no measurable benefit, and the network did
        not reach the pre-registered bar for learning which action helps in which state.
  \item \textbf{Bandit layer.} Thompson sampling over operators was never better than trying
        untried options systematically, and in the final round it was slower.
  \item \textbf{Curiosity Q-learning.} It reliably triggered more distinct browser errors, on
        the development applications and on the external benchmark, but verified fewer
        seeded faults.
\end{itemize}
This agrees with the observation from Humanoid that a learned model may not be the main
reason for a tester's gains~\cite{li2019humanoid}. It also agrees with the web-testing study
that found coverage only weakly related to failure revelation~\cite{liu2026webgui}: the arm
that causes the most errors is not the one that verifies the most faults.

\paragraph{How the tester compares with the literature.} On WebExplor's own applications and
measures, the frozen tester did not reach WebExplor's or WebRLED's published coverage or
failure counts. The post-hoc follow-up located a large part of the gap in the action space,
not the exploration policy. With custom clickables, Phoenix coverage rose to WebRLED's
level for every arm, and over the five applications other than Petclinic the arms matched or
exceeded WebExplor's average, though not WebRLED's. The pre-registered second round, with
listener-based detection and five repetitions, gave the same picture: the curiosity arm
averaged 53.6\% over six applications, just below WebExplor, and 55.1\% without Petclinic,
between WebExplor and WebRLED. It still triggered fewer console failures than either.
Whatever policy a tester uses, it can act only on the elements it detects. The comparison
has its own limits: three repetitions instead of five in the first round, different
machines, automatic instead of manual failure deduplication, and published numbers we could
not re-run.

\paragraph{Verified faults and coverage disagree.} The project's own evaluations measure
verified seeded faults; the literature measures coverage and console errors. The hybrid was
designed and tuned for the first, and is strongest there. On the second it is close to
random on coverage and below the published testers on failures. A tester for real use needs
both: deep, verified functional faults, and broad reach over the interface.

Arms that learn to explore, curiosity Q-learning and the deep Q-network, do best on the
literature's measures: breadth of coverage and errors triggered.
The structure without a learner does best on verified seeded faults, which require
deliberate test actions rather than novelty. No learning layer added on top of the structure
verified clearly more faults than the structure alone: at best one more on the held-out
applications (19 against 18), and fewer in tuning. Since WebExplor and WebRLED are both RL
testers, the like-for-like comparison is with \code{hybrid\_curiosity}. It reached
44.7\% branch coverage; with custom clickables it reached 54.1\% over all six applications,
and 53.6\% in the pre-registered second round: close to WebExplor (55.5\%) and below WebRLED
(64.2\%).

\paragraph{Limits of the evidence.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale.} Every evaluation used six to eight applications, which leaves wide
        intervals; all four held-out comparisons were inconclusive.
  \item \textbf{Same developer.} The development and validation applications were built
        within the project. The external applications were not, and there the tester did
        worse.
  \item \textbf{Seeded faults.} Seeded faults stand in for real ones only
        partially.
  \item \textbf{Post-hoc analyses.} The failure measure comparable with the papers, the
        clickables follow-up and the view without Petclinic were defined after the results
        and are reported as such.
  \item \textbf{Phoenix cold start.} On a freshly started container, Phoenix's first sign-in
        redirect can complete after the macro's wait, so a run's first episode may begin on
        the sign-in page. This affects at most one episode per run, equally for every arm.
\end{itemize}
